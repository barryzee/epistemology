Motivation
The discussion in the interview [2] is about Francis’ book, which is entitled The Language of God: A Scientist Presents Evidence for Belief . This discussion is expected to be of immense interest to my epistemology project.
Francis Collins is uniquely qualified as an educated, knowledgeable, articulate, thoughtful, logical, rational spokesperson. Until recently, he was the director of the premier bio- medical research facility in the world, namely the National Institutes of Health. He was also the originator of the Human Genome Project, that resulted in the first nearly complete sequence of the entire human genome, an accomplishment equivalent to sending a manned spaceship to the moon. We have the words of a foremost scientist and Evangelical presenting evidence for belief in god and religion. I emphasize evidence because too often we see mere subjective opinion by unqualified individuals.
The hugely important task of eliciting the expert evidence fell to Terry Gross, the host of Fresh Air. Her job was to keep Francis focused on the question at hand: namely providing expert evidence, making sure that he is responsive to each key question, and that his evidence was stated clearly and unambiguously enough that the audience could be fully enlightened.

LOGIC
Use of Logic in Considering Evidence for Belief in God and Religion
At around 9:50 in the interview, Francis states
But a scientist who says “Well science is sufficient to disprove god because we haven’t caught him in our microscopes or our other means of measurements” is committing a logical fallacy.
Of course, Francis is correct. For instance, in principle it may be that god exists, but that the existence of god is not measurable or observable by any scientific experiment to date (or perhaps ever). Or it could be that the correct experiment to detect god has not yet been performed. Or perhaps the correct experiment has been performed, but the results were not analyzed properly, or not reported to the public.
So (other scientists’) claims of atheism are probably as (logically) inappropriate as claims of theism. A logically reasonable position at this time is agnosticism, or perhaps just ignoring the question entirely and having no opinion. After all, we are not obligated to analyze and have an opinion about every single thing that some human has posited throughout the course of history. Many such suppositions are without merit, and we cannot spend all of our waking moments evaluating each utterance through all of time.
10
Here is the real value of Francis’ pointing out the logical fallacy. Francis has established that logic can and should be used in dealing with religious matters and in deciding about the belief in god. In particular, Francis himself and the rest of us must avoid introducing fallacies when discussing the religious belief or the existence of god. To paraphrase Perry Mason
Mr. Burger, you have opened the door, and now I can step through
So now that Francis has established that logic can and should be used in dealing with religious belief and in deciding about the existence of god, we should be a little more systematic in listing specific fallacies that we need to look out for, and to make sure that we do not produce them, and that we do not accept them in other people’s arguments. Fortunately, the study of fallacies has been extensive, and common fallacies have even been given names. We certainly do not want to run afoul of listed and named fallacies. Atheism as a Red Herring
The question of atheism is discussed to some extent, but that is a red herring. People who embrace atheism are, in their own way, jumping to definite (but unknowable) conclusions in much the same way as people who embrace religion. It probably makes more sense to be agnostic, that is, to realize that evidence is not available one way or the other. One must give up the comfort of a certainty for the discomfort of ambiguity.
A cogent argument that people are over-reaching when they embrace atheism is by no means the same thing as presenting evidence for belief in god or religion. Rather, it is a distraction from presenting such evidence.

NO EVIDENCE IS PRESENTED
What might Constitute Evidence for Belief in God and Religion?
So, the real task is to parse the 37 minute discussion to focus on those portions in which Francis actually does present evidence for belief in god or religion. Of course, we do not expect to find something like a mathematical proof. If such a proof were to exist, then there would be no room for people to be agnostic or atheist. We will be content with some evidence, short of proof, that might motivate belief in god or religion.
For instance, prior to 1927 or so, the age of the Earth was estimated to be some several thousands of years old. This would not have provided enough time for evolution of humans to occur, so at that time there might have been some reason to believe in some sort of direct quick creation of species or of humans. Of course, an unbiased person at that time might have been concerned that the young estimate of the age of the Earth might eventually prove to be incorrect, rather than latching onto it in order to believe in god. And that explanation still would be obligated to explain how something as powerful as god, that had the power to directly create humans, came about. If you want to say that god came about in a
17
mysterious way, then you might as well say that humans came about in the same mysterious way, and dispense with god entirely.
But after 1927, the real age of the Earth was estimated more accurately, and the young Earth basis for believing in god was no longer in play. Gilbert and Sullivan could have composed a libretto about the Earth aging by several billion years in the single “leap” year 1927.
So, we are looking for Francis to present some kind of evidence, analogous to the young age of the Earth before 1927, that could motivate a reason for belief in god and religion. That is, we are hoping for something more tangible than simply an emotional appeal, or that believing in god makes your life seem more fulfilling etc .
So, the question is, does Francis provide logical evidence that would differentiate whether god exists or not, in external reality? It would be nice if he could present proof, but even evidence would be of value. I am not optimistic, as an awful lot of the interview, to be frank, consists of fluff, and if he had real evidence he would not waste his time presenting fluff. This is just a suggestive hint, but it does have the unfortunate consequence of setting a poor expectation.
The Origin, Intention, Content and Degree of Literalness of the Bible
At around 13:30, Francis says that
18
genesis was not intended as a scientific textbook
At around 15:40, he goes on to say that
a literal interpretation of genesis I and II is almost certainly unsustainable when you look at the scientific evidence that has been accumulated from cosmology and geology and physics and chemistry and biology
By these two statements, Francis strongly suggest that genesis was written by a person or persons, not by god. Furthermore, the person(s) had a particular intention that was non- literal. The most reasonable alternative is that the intention was to provide a metaphor, or an invention. This example provides a good illustration of the principles I espoused in the paragraph “Motivation” in the chapter “INVENTION”
As humans, we sometimes invent a concept, and, after a period of time, we forget that it was invented. Instead, we mistakenly think of it as real – that is, as existing in and of itself, outside of our own minds.
Now if, as Francis suggests, genesis is to be considered as a non-literal metaphor or invention, then there is no reason to believe that other portions of the rest of the bible should not also be considered as a non-literal metaphor or invention. Of course, some portions of the bible do contain real and independently-verifiable historical events. So, the entire bible is a mixture of real events seamlessly blended with non-literal metaphor or invention. Since
19
we are engaging in guesses about the intentions of the persons who wrote these, my guess is that
They were written by highly educated enlightened and well-intentioned individuals. These individuals saw the morals of their metaphors and inventions as being important for the well-being of their fellow humans. They also saw that imparting them with the weight of a higher being would make it more likely that people would pay attention to them.
It is anyone’s guess as to whether they realized how successful a societal cultural meme they were creating. Phenomena such as religious bigotry, the holocaust, pedophilia in the Catholic Church, and many others, serve to caution us about the unintended consequences of a well-intentioned metaphorical invention, namely the enormous amount of harm that can ensue when metaphor is misinterpreted too rigidly.

Why am I bringing this up? Well, if a person is speaking about their beliefs, it would be a good idea to repeat the phrase “It is my personal subjective belief that . . .” That way, the listener could not confuse a statement of personal subjective belief as being an assertion about external reality. It may become tedious to prepend this phrase before each sentence, but, in the long run, it is preferable to undergo this tedium rather than for the listener to confuse personal subjective belief with external reality. In the rare event that the listener could be trusted to remember that context (as the trained judge in the Nero Wolfe clip) then the speaker could make a one-time explicit disclaimer that could apply to the remainder of that particular discussion.
Francis himself recognizes that none of what he says constitutes evidence to support belief in god or religion. Being an educated and nuanced person, Francis understands that his statements represent personal subjective belief rather than objective reality. In order to avoid misleading or confusing his audience (who, after all, may not be as educated and nuanced as Francis is), Francis should prepend a disclaimer before each statement of personal subjective belief, or at least clearly make a one-time explicit disclaimer that could apply to the remainder of the particular discussion. It would be best if the title of this interview did not contain the word “evidence” if he does not in fact provide any evidence.
