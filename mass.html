MASS
Say that we had a helium balloon. If we release it, it does not fall towards the ground. If we give it a little push, it zooms all over the place. This is pretty frustrating, as we are accustomed to things dropping or reacting modestly to a little push.
But a solution is at hand. I happen to have a little bottle of tiny brakes. If I sprinkle some on the balloon, now when I give it a little push, it just moves a little bit. But it still does not fall towards the ground.
Fortunately, I happen to have another little bottle of tiny gravity detectors. If I sprinkle some on the balloon, now it falls towards the ground.
So, I have the two components that make an object behave as expected, one for giving it some inertial resistance to a push, and one for making it fall under the influence of gravity.
But there is something wrong with my story. In the real world, you cannot add or take away a desired amount of little brakes or little gravity detectors. Each real object comes with these prepackaged, and they cannot be added or subtracted. And they always come together in the exact same proportions, no matter what substance the object is made of. So, there are not two separate things like little brakes and little gravity detectors, there is just one mystery substance that comprises both of these.
This narrative is a bit of a simplification, just to keep the story more readable. More realistically, each item like inertial mass or gravitational mass might need three internal components: a signal detector, a responder, and an adapter (that couples the detected signal to the proper response of the responder). The inertial force and gravitational detectors could be distinct, but the responder(s) would need to be the same. See also the section ‘Brain as a Mechanical Linkage or as an Instrument of Free Will?’ in the chapter ‘The Limbic System.’
I am of such an age that when I took science in school, we learned about electrons and protons, but not about quarks. No matter what substance an
object is made of, if you dig deep enough, its protons and neutrons are made of quarks held together by gluons (more or less). This is the mystery substance. It is no coincidence that Superman was called Quark Kent.
If it were not for this, objects of different weight would not hit the ground at the same time when dropped from the Leaning Tower of Pisa. And Einstein’s Theory of General Relativity would not exist, because (in technical terms) the inertial mass would not be identical to the gravitational mass.
At this point in time, it is believed that there is no finer subdivision of the quark. I am not sure if this is a statement about the fundamental nature of reality, or a statement about the current technical limitations of particle accelerators.
I do not want to overwork the little bottle of tiny gravity detectors analogy, but there is yet one more component that quarks need to be bestowed with – namely generating gravity. Not only are inertial mass and gravitational mass one and the same, but generating gravity is one and the same as responding to gravity, and therefore it is also one and the same as inertial mass.
I am trying to keep this discussion at a fairly high level of abstraction, but I will mention that ‘generating gravity’ might mean any one of several things: (1) graviton particles, (2) gravity waves, (3) curving space-time, (4) abstract mathematical field, etc. , depending on which theory of gravity you prefer.
This means that if you have 2 quarks (and their gluons) separated by millions of miles, each one will generate a gravitational force that the other will respond to. Now when my iPhone is searching for a possible signal of an incoming call, the battery gets used up and I need to recharge every so often. How can a lowly quark search for possible gravitational signals from a humungous number of quarks spread all over the universe, and not run out of power? And at the same time, it must generate its own gravitational signal, and not run out of power from that?
As an aside, what is the exact nature of the gravitational signal? Since it can be carried by a vacuum, it is obviously not a traditional material object. Scientists circumvent this dilemma by inventing the concept of a ‘field,’ which is used in sentences as if it is something that really exists but when forced to answer, it is admitted that a field is nothing more than an abstract mathematical construct. This then gets us back to where we were a hundred years ago, with some mysterious non-substance that used to be called the ‘aether.’ I developed an emotional dislike of aether, since it always seemed like a pure invention to explain things could not be explained, and I disliked the ‘ae’ part of the word, at least go ahead and call it ‘ether.’ ‘Aether’ sounds too much like what an alchemist would call something.
This behavior of a single quark is more like the behavior of a computer that is plugged into a power source in a space ship. Just imagine a computer that detects zillions of zillions of zillions of gravity signals coming from all over the universe, then feeding that data to a program that figures out the net result of how to respond. But by the time this is computed, everything has changed and the next computation must start. Of course, you can never keep up, all you can do is fall further and further behind, and lose more and more data. Pretty much like Lucy and Ethel working on the conveyor belt in the candy factory. Just grab what you can and shove it down your blouse.
But someone might argue that my analogy with a computer is too extreme. Here is a description of the simplest receiver, namely a ‘crystal set.’ But note that this can only detect signals, it cannot move itself in response to the signals.
Now when the gravitational signal is detected, the quark does not get exact information on where the signal came from. Instead, it gets the result of superimposed signals along a ‘line integral.’ In biomedical imaging, such superimposed signals must be processed by feeding the computer multiple sets of signals that are recorded at every angle for 360 degrees surrounding the source. Only then can the individual signals be computationally estimated (and that with a lot of noise and uncertainty). But our quark only has information from its point of view, not from 360 degrees. And the quark does not have a computer at its disposal. So how does its precious gravitational mass know what to do?
An alternative might be for each gravitational signal to encode its originating location, but that is not feasible because, according to the Special Theory of Relativity, there is no universal absolute reference frame, so there would be no possibility of encoding (or decoding) an absolute location.
The chance that the human brain will ever understand is even more hopeless, as the quarks do so much more than impart mass. They are the
core feature of both protons and neutrons, and by definition they give each atomic element its identity and properties.
But now I have to admit that I have misled you. This chapter is not about mass at all. It is about the unreliability of the human brain.
It is my contention that the human brain will never be able to explain or comprehend this chapter, although gravity undoubtedly exists and happens. Very gifted physicists will propose ever more ingenious theories, but they are destined to fail.
The real solution is to recognize that the human brain has some very real limits, and those limits need to be respected in any discussion that tries to figure out something that is as complicated as gravity.
There is one more aspect of ‘modern’ physics that is equally inaccessible, and that, of course, is quantum theory, be it old fashioned quantum mechanics or more sophisticated quantum field theory and its offshoots. I am not a physicist, and my intention is to do nothing more than to mention gravity and quantum field theory as two areas that (1) underlie our material reality, (2) form the basis of how our brains function, and (3) will likely never be understood by the human brain.
These shortcomings severely compromise everything else that I say in this manuscript. To relate all of this to epistemology, we must be willing to leave our egotism on the trash heap, and recognize that our brains are ok for those things that ‘determined’ their survivability ( i.e , biological evolution), but not to extrapolate beyond that.
Unfortunately, we run into the double problem: the human brain is limited, AND humans have too big an ego to recognize and act on this. And many bad things happen when we rigidly project our incorrect subjective desires upon external reality, and upon other humans.
Here is a riddle for extra credit: We know that the sun exerts enormous gravitational attraction for Earth; after all Earth is huge, and this huge object is kept in orbit around the sun. So why is it that a person falls to Earth but not to the sun? How does the sun hold onto something as huge as Earth, but seems to have no effect on a tiny human?
The same could be said of the moon, its pull is so strong as to cause tides, influencing zillions of tons of sea water, but does not have any effect on a tiny human?
Shameful Self-Indulgent Speculation
There is a certain food chain of existence. Macroscopic things are made up of smaller things, and so forth. Does this keep going without end, or is there an end at some point ( e.g., quarks)?
Beings that have some degree of consciousness are going to be near the top of the food chain. After all, you need to have some components in order to harness, store, organize, retrieve, and use energy and information. As you descend down the food chain, the denizens of that universe might be totally unlike anything in your macroscopic environment. Even though we are made up of things like quarks, the composition and behavior and rules of quarks are totally unrelated to our level of everyday reality. It is inevitable that the deeper you dig, the more impossible it is to relate to what you find.
The illusion we have of stability and stationarity is built upon a complex system of planetary motion and physical laws (that we partially have access to and knowledge of). It did not have to be that way. It could be that we are constantly aware of the crazy speed and ride of Earth’s motion. I think we would be very dizzy and distracted if all of this did not somehow ‘cancel’ out. Every step that we take, we would worry about a million things, including whether gravity would work properly in that location. How could we concentrate on, say, solving a simple equation, if everything is spinning around and we are in chaos?
But the underlying things, that provide us with the illusion of stability, may themselves be quite inexplicable to us. It may very well be inevitable that, whenever there is such a thing as a food chain, the illusions that benefit that top part are based in hidden details below that are inaccessible to understanding.
Our everyday existence is based upon the unexplainable (to our brain) behavior of gravity and quantum phenomena. When you do the most common of everyday activities, such as you get into your car to drive to the grocery store, you do not think about it, but there is an enormous infrastructure that makes this possible in a seamless manner. There are people that plan the city streets, people who pave the city street, people who pay the people who do those jobs, people who feed the people who do those jobs, a fairly repressive economic system (be it capitalism or communism) that provides the compulsion for people to do those jobs in order to get paid, etc, etc . Without all of those components, and many others, you would not be able to hop in your car and go to the grocery store. What we take for granted as our everyday existence in this physical world depends on an infrastructure of gravity and quantum phenomena, that we usually do not want to be bothered with (except perhaps for physicists).
Epilogue
In a machine shop, there are many kinds of power tools, saws, lathes, sanders, etc. Each tool has its own use, and you would use the appropriate tool for the job you needed done. You would never expect a power saw to sand a piece of wood.
The human brain is but a single tool in a shop. It works ok for certain concrete jobs. But you should not ask the brain to do an abstract job that requires a different tool. If you do, it might do the best it can, but that best might be very far from what the (hypothetical) correct tool would do.
There is no shame in recognizing the limitations of the human brain; the shame is the ignorance, arrogance, and hubris of not doing so.
