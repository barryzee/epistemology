THE MATHEMATICAL STATISTICAL INVENTORY OF RELIGION
Definition and Inventory
Based on [1]:
While the word religion is difficult to define and understand, one standard model of religion that is used in religious studies courses defines it as
[a] system of symbols which acts to establish powerful, pervasive, and long-lasting moods and motivations in men by formulating conceptions of a general order of existence and clothing these conceptions with such an aura of factuality that the moods and motivations seem uniquely realistic.
Many religions have their own narratives, symbols, traditions and sacred histories that are intended to give meaning to life or to explain the origin of life or the universe. They tend to derive morality, ethics, religious laws, or a preferred lifestyle from their ideas about the cosmos and human nature. According to some estimates, there are roughly 4,200 religions, churches, denominations, religious bodies, faith groups, tribes, cultures, movements, or ultimate concerns.
Regarding the figure of 4,200, there are two aspects that are best to get out of the way immediately, assuming the curators of the 4,200 list was doing their job correctly:
• If any 2 religions had been essentially identical, then, when the inventory was taken, presumably they would have been combined into 1 and not double-counted. Thus, we assume that the 4,200 religions comprise a unique set of mutually exclusive religions.
• If any one had contained a tenet that is demonstrably false ( e.g. , “the age of the Earth is 5,000 years”), then presumably that religion would have been discarded and not taken to count towards the 4,200 total.
It is noteworthy that the standard definition characterized religion as a human-created narrative, and that there are several thousand religions, although as we know, there are only around five that comprise most of the peoples in the word today.
God may or may not exist in external objective reality, and it is likely that humans will never have objective knowledge (caution not to confuse a strong subjective belief with objective knowledge ).
In the (likely) event that god does not exist in external objective reality, then all of the 4,200 are incorrect, as they each incorrectly depend on their own concept of what god is, but there is no god, so they are all wrong.
On the other hand, in the (unlikely) event that god does exist in external objective reality, then one of the 4,200 may have hit the nail on the head. If so, only that one was correct, as the 4,200 are presumably mutually- exclusive. Of course, it is more likely that none of the 4,200 are correct (after all, do you really think that the human brain could intuit the underlying reality of a god, by random luck; god could be something totally different than our guesses, or may be so different than our everyday experience that we would never imagine the correct answer)?
A Skewed Distribution
Given that there are 4,200 religions, that are all equally possible, it is surprising that there are only around 5 that comprise most of humanity. Assuming that the world’s population is around 7 billion, we would expect them to be divided into around 7 billion/4,200 or around 1.67 million people per religion. So why is the actual distribution so distorted from the expected?
Well, to achieve the expected distribution, each person would need to have unfettered access to each possible religion. And then to pick and choose among these, based on personal preference (ideally after a substantial amount of independent thought and life experience). That is the fair way for religions to compete in the global marketplace of ideas. But why is it that this does not happen in the correct way?
