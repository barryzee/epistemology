Does “now” really exist in external reality, or is “now” simply a human concept or invention, perhaps of a psychological nature?
The concept of “Dedekind Cut” depends in the underlying entity being continuous rather than discrete. But it is not known whether time is continuous, or discrete or quantized [2]. Is there a smallest “unit” of time?

Is There Such A Thing in Physical Reality As “Present” or Is That Just an Invention of the Brain (Human or Otherwise)?
As described in [8] our sense of “present” is an elaborate illusion constructed by the brain. There are a number of roughly overlapping threads of processing going on at different speeds that result in a composite picture of “present.” Coupled with ongoing subconscious neural mechanisms to back-fill and predict/extrapolate, we are tricked into experiencing a seamless continuity of events.
Many of the references used in this chapter describe the timing of sequential processes that are required in order to integrate an initial vision or experience into our consciousness. There are two related questions that occur to me, that I have not seen explicitly discussed in my (admittedly superficial) reading:
Question 1
Say that it might take 13 msec in order to complete the initial perception of a scene. Do we need to wait until that 13 msec is over in order to process another scene, or can we perceive another scene after waiting say 1 msec, and both of those are in a sort of queue being processed in a partially over-lapping manner? If so, what is the minimum time between them (I just made up the 1
14
msec, maybe it is really a million times faster than this?)? There must be some limit, or else the brain would need to process and keep track of an arbitrarily large number of such threads, which cannot be done with a finite number of neurons.
Question 2
Although the initial perception might take 13 msec, subsequent processing of that same event might take 100 or 200 msec. So does the brain start say 10 threads of processing, 13 msec apart, and then partially overlap the 200 msec processing to complete each of these? Or does the brain need to wait for the 200 msec to be over before starting the initial perception of the “next” scene? This latter scenario is more reasonable, since in the first scenario the 200 msec process would keep falling further and further behind, with no chance of catching up (a little bit like the US national debt).

The present has finite thickness?:
If the present has finite thickness, then presumably the cross- section of that thickness is either uniform (Figure 7B) or non- uniform (Figure 7C).
In the next section, we explore the imaginative (and highly speculative) possibility that past and future are physically different from one another. That is, that they are potentially distinguishable from one another (using certain hypothetical instrumentation that has yet to be developed).
If the present is uniform throughout its entire finite thickness, then presumably there is a discontinuous boundary between past and future at trailing and leading edges, respectively (Figure 7B). I guess these edges would be infinitely thin in the sense of a “Dedekind Cut.”

If the present is non-uniform across its thickness, perhaps the cross-section tapers from past-like to future-like, continuously merging into past and future at both edges (Figure 7C).
An interesting aspect of the models in which present has finite thickness is that the length of the thickness is the amount of time that it takes for the future to become the past. There is an interesting self-referentiality to this statement, since we are talking about how fast time flows, but time is the thing that we use against which to measure how fast something happens. How can we use time to measure how fast time flows?
