LIGHT AND THE ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM IN THE CONTEXT OF THE EVOLUTION OF THE HUMAN BRAIN
MOTIVATION
The motivation for this essay is to provide a specific example of a general aspect of biological evolution, mentioned in a passage in a previous section “TUTORIAL ON GENES, MUTATIONS, AND EVOLUTION.” Here is a passage [1] taken from that section [emphasis mine], that is essential for understanding the concepts in my manuscript:
Of course, fitness is a relative thing. A genotype's
fitness depends on the environment in which the
organism lives. The fittest genotype during an ice age, for example, is probably not the fittest genotype once the ice age is over.
Fitness is a handy concept because it lumps everything that matters to natural selection (survival, mate-finding, reproduction) into one idea. The fittest individual is not
necessarily the strongest, fastest, or biggest. A
genotype's fitness includes its ability to survive, find a
mate, produce offspring — and ultimately leave its
genes in the next generation.
That is, the successful object of biological evolution is that object which has survival value relative to the given environment. It is not an object that functions perfectly in the absolute sense of a Platonic ideal. The operational word is relative rather than absolute .
The perception of the electromagnetic spectrum by the human retina is a perfect example of relative versus absolute .
LIGHT AND THE ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM
Evolution has endowed the human retina with the ability to see but a small portion of the electromagnetic spectrum (EM spectrum). Other familiar portions of the EM spectrum include x- rays and microwaves. Excellent discussions are provided in references [2] – [6]. The very interesting history of the discovery of other parts of the EM spectrum is given in [6]. This underscores how difficult it has been for humans to understand that there was a whole lot more
out there, beyond the limited scope that evolution allowed us to directly sense. Here is a brief portion of that history [6]:
For most of history, visible light was the only known part of the electromagnetic spectrum.
. . .
The first discovery of
electromagnetic radiation
other than visible light came in 1800, when
William Herschel
discovered
infrared
radiation. He was studying the temperature of different colors by moving a thermometer through light split by a prism. He noticed that the highest temperature was beyond red. He theorized that this temperature change was due to "calorific rays", a type of light ray that could not be seen.
The next year,
Johann Ritter
, working at the other end of the spectrum, noticed what he called "chemical rays" (invisible light rays that induced certain chemical reactions). These behaved similarly to visible violet light rays, but were beyond them in the spectrum. They were later renamed
ultraviolet
radiation.
The point is that evolution provided a visual system that has survival value relative to the given environment. It is not an object that functions perfectly in the absolute sense, in that only a very limited portion of EM spectrum is detected by our visual system.
If our visual system had been more perfect in an absolute sense, then it would have been very deficient in survival value relative to the given environment. Imagine that we need to detect a lion that is threatening us. Then all of the information contained in EM spectrum, other than the visible portion, would constitute noise. Our brain would need to spend considerable time filtering away all of this noise in order to recognize and react to the threat.
GENERALIZATION
As interesting as the topic of perception of light by the human retina is, I am mentioning this as a particular example of a generalization about the human brain. The human brain is an object that is the result of biological evolution. So just as the retina is an object that has survival value relative to the given environment, so too is the human brain. That is, the human brain is not an object that functions perfectly in the absolute sense.
As important as this realization is on a theoretical basis, it has enormous consequences in everyday practical terms. First and foremost, it tells us that our thoughts and perceptions may not be nearly as reliable as we typically regard them. There are several difficulties with accepting this:
• The argument is self-referential, in that we are using the human brain, which by hypothesis is severely flawed, in order to examine the flaws in the human brain. Ideally, we would use a much more reliable tool for performing this examination. Unfortunately, the human brain is the only tool that we have available.
• Evolution has endowed us not only with a flawed brain, but also with an incredibly strong ulterior motive to regard our brain and perception as functioning essentially perfectly in the absolute sense. So, we are not inclined to entertain these examinations.
• Most, if not all, human cultures and societies would need to be re-evaluated and re-built from the ground up if we were
to properly account for the flaws in the human brain and perception. However, this is one of those things where it can be said that, in the long run, it is more expensive not to make the changes than to make them.
• Whereas the teacher (in the true sense of the word) would seek to educate , literally to lead forth from the flawed way of thinking, the demagogue/tyrant seeks to reinforce and manipulate and exploit the flawed way of thinking for his own ends.
