HUMAN TIME SCALES
We are naturally aware of things that are happening a little later today. If something is happening next week, or later in the year, we most likely need to make an entry in calendar schedule. It is well within our everyday comfort level to think of eat ing lunch soon.
But our existence is also predicated on the speed with which our visual system can process what we see (which is happening on the hundred millisecond range). We are lucky this is happening without our conscious participation, as we cannot attend to this several times a second, all day long. Not to mention our metabolism, largely governed by enzymes, perhaps at the nanosecond range. We can write down a numerical expression for that time range, but we do not really intuit what is means (except perhaps a physicist or biochemist working with this as a career).
On the other extreme, we have things like the age of the Earth, measured in the billions of years. Again, we have no intuition of what this really means. Which is why it is hard for the average person to grasp that this amount of time allows for humans to have evolved, and mistakenly think of this as a ‘miracle,’ or as ‘designed.’ There was a time when it was mistakenly believed that the age of the Earth is much younger, and, given this error, it would not have been unreasonable to conclude that there would not have been enough time for evolution to occur.
Two of the things that facilitate our intuitive sense of time are the rotation of Earth (once per day, by definition), and Earth orbiting the Sun (once per year, by definition). Many things that we do are based on a day, including behavior and biological functions .
For example, we eat 2 or 3 times a day. That is not exactly once a day, but on the other hand, it could have been that we needed to eat 1,000 times a day, or once a year. We tend to defecate once a day. We sleep once a day. We brush our teeth once or twice a day. A newly-wed might have sex once a day (more or less).

It might not be surprising that things we need to consciously tend to happen once a day. After all, we evolved on a planet that had daily cycles of light and dark, so we are adapted to that rhythm.
But in a way it is surprising. After all, our bodies require a certain amount of energy to function, and our metabolism needs to provide that energy, largely from food that we eat and oxygen that we breathe. It could have been that food had a lower caloric and nutritional content, so that we would need to eat all day long, and even then still be in deficit. It could have been that the size of the Earth was much smaller, or the numerical value for the universal gravitational constant could have been much smaller. In either case, the amount of oxygen in the Earth’s atmosphere would be smaller, and perhaps our hemoglobin would not be able to deliver the amount of oxygen that we require. This could have severe consequences on the amount of glucose that we synthesize, thus impairing our brain function, or perhaps preventing the evolution of our brain in the first place.
It takes a reasonable fraction of a day to eat, to have sex, to get dressed – does not take one nanosecond, does not take one year.

In addition to how often we do certain things, we can also consider how long we spend doing these things. Obviously, if we need to do, say, five things in a day, each thing cannot take more than an hour or two. Let us take, for instance, having sex. If that were to take only a few nanoseconds, it would not be worth the effort. If it took a year, no one would have that kind of time to spend on it.
So as a result of a long chain of events, a newly-wed bride will be happy or upset depending on whether her husband has sex with her at a frequency that that can be traced directly back to the angular momentum of the primordial cloud of dust.
