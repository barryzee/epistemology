MECHANISM FOR THE PERPETUATION OF RELIGIOUS BELIEF
As discussed in chapter ‘INVENTION,’ religious belief is an invention of the human brain. It persists as a meme through time, geography, and cultures. It is of interest to try to ascertain the mechanism for the persistence of religious belief, both as a generalized phenomenon and in the specifics of a particular religious dogma.
As mentioned in chapter ‘TIME-LINE OF THE ORIGIN AND CORRUPTION OF RELIGIOUS THOUGHT,’ contemporary Catholic dogma is rooted in the shameful self-serving demagoguery of the Medieval church. The specific Catholic dogma from the Medieval remarkably is retained to this day.
I am reminded of the party game, popular decades ago, called “telephone.” The first player whispers a message to the second player, and so on. By the end of the chain, the original message is inevitably totally garbled.
How is it that Catholic identity and dogma from the Medieval are so faithfully retained to this day? A major part of the answer can be gleaned from a section entitled “Links between childhood religious upbringing and current religious identity” in a report by the Pew Research Center [1]. These apply to families in the U.S.
I have reproduced two tables that appear in that report. The tables show how often (adult) children have certain religious beliefs, based on the religious beliefs of their parents. For instance, in the
first table, we see that a child raised by two Catholic parents will be Catholic 62% of the time, Protestant 16% of the time, and
unaffiliated 19% of the time. Similarly, in the second table, we see that a child raised by two unaffiliated parents will be unaffiliated 63% of the time.
There is nothing special about Catholicism that generates a familial loyalty, as unaffiliated families pass that on with the same degree of fidelity as do the Catholic families. It must just be that newborn infants and young children do not have the capacity to think for themselves, and so are “hoodwinked” by their very own parents into buying into the parents’ belief system, for better or for worse. And these children, having been thus “influenced,” will grow up to then “influence” their own children, for better or for worse.
So, children who eventually grow up and adopt Catholicism (or unaffiliated) are not doing so because Catholicism (or unaffiliated) is “correct” in any sense, they are doing so because they were “brainwashed” into it by someone who they trusted implicitly and who had total control over their thoughts, before they were old enough to think for themselves.
The figure of 62% retention of Catholicism could be analyzed by various statistical tests of the type called “contingency tests.” The results of such analyses would show that the correlation between two Catholic parents and Catholic belief for the child is astronomically significant, way different from what would be expected by chance. Oftentimes it is difficult to determine cause and effect from correlation data, but in this case it is clear – the parents’ belief influences the child’s belief, not vice versa . So, this goes a long way towards explaining the persistence of religious belief – it is culturally passed on from parent to child
(see Table 2 in the chapter ‘EPISTEMOLOGY TABLES.’ The entry ‘CULTURAL/AMBIENT REPETITION’ jumps out in this regard, especially ‘TRUSTED AUTHORITY FIGURES’ like ‘PARENTS.’)
However, 62% is not 100%, and this 38% degree of leakage would be expected to lead to fewer and fewer Catholics after a few generations. But from the second table, we can see that a certain percentage of children raised by two unaffiliated parents will be Christian or Catholic. So, these might compensate to some extent for the 38% Catholic leakage.
It is not surprising that some children would be able to throw off the yoke of their parents’ dogma, and become unaffiliated. However, it is more surprising to me that the children of unaffiliated parents would adopt something so specific as the entire set of Christian or Catholic dogma. This must indicate that there is some secondary mechanism in U.S. culture, in conjunction with a core genomic drive to religiosity (see chapter ‘INVENTION’) that is effective, but not as effective as parental influence. Perhaps in a largely Christian society, unaffiliated people feel marginalized, and they want to do what they must in order to attain membership in the club? Or maybe it is purely the core genomic drive?
