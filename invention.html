Motivation
As humans, we sometimes invent a concept, and, after a period of time, we forget that it was invented. Instead, we mistakenly think of it as real – that is, as existing in and of itself, outside of our own minds.
The original invention might have occurred quite recently, or many years ago.
This can happen even to the very same person who was the inventor. Perhaps more commonly, it might happen to other people who might be familiar with the concept, but not familiar with how or when it was invented.
The invented concept might appear to be very useful to ( i.e., “serves”) some individuals. For instance, the concept might be of practical utility in commerce, or it might be of personal utility in organizing thoughts about how to more comfortably live life, or it might provide an alternative subjective option to an otherwise unpalatable reality relating to our own existence. These attributes might tend to make the invented concept more enduring ( i.e. , a “meme”) in the social or cultural group, or perhaps throughout most of humanity.
The invented concept might, by chance, align with some aspects of reality, or it might be entirely divorced from reality, but still have some aura that makes it attractive to a critical mass of individuals.
Some Examples from Physics and Math
Time
Until the early part of the 20 th century, Isaac Newton’s view of time was commonly accepted:
Absolute, true and mathematical time, of itself, and from its own nature flows equably without regard to anything external [3].
Einstein and his contemporaries eventually showed that the passage of time depends on circumstance and environment [emphasis mine] [ 3]. That is,
Time does NOT flow equably without regard to anything external.
Sets
Georg Cantor
, one of the founders of set theory, gave the following
definition of a set at the beginning of his Beiträge zur Begründung der transfiniten Mengenlehre :
A set is a gathering together into a whole of definite, distinct objects of our perception [ Anschauung ] or of our thought — which are called elements of the set [emphasis mine] [4].
Functions
A relation is a set of ordered pairs [5].
A function is a relation for which each value from the set the first
components of the ordered pairs is associated with exactly one value from
the set of second components of the ordered pair [5].
Since a relation and a function are sets, they are objects of our perception or of our thought .
“Working Definition” of Function
A function is an equation for which any x that can be plugged into the equation will yield exactly one y out of the equation.
Combining the Concepts of Time and Functions via Graphs
Anyone who has taken an introductory physics class has come across graphs of distance versus time. That is, time is represented on the x-axis, and distance is represented on the y-axis. A typical example is shown in Figure 1 [6].

Other types of graphs
Another type of graph is the histogram (Figure 2). Histograms essentially group the obtained data into sets, known as bins [7].
Figure 2. Histogram of frequency of people’s heights
Although all of the people of a certain height range are grouped together into a single bin, these people are not really adjacent to one another – some may be in the U.S., and others in the same bin might be in China. No one would think that, just because the graph shows them together in the same bin, these people are actually standing together in a clump.
The example of the histogram shows us that we cannot make the mistake of thinking that what we see in graphs (of types like histograms) is anything other than an abstraction of the arrangement that exists in reality.
This is fundamentally different from the distance versus time graph, in which the time points that are adjacent in the graph are universally regarded as actually being adjacent in the real world.
Vision
As suggested in the quotation at the beginning of this essay, vision is a virtual construct created by the brain. It makes sense to discuss vision in the context of the other topics mentioned above, namely time, sets, functions, and graphs. That is because these topics, and vision, share the property of being virtual constructs created by the brain.
The neurobiology of vision involves the decomposition of the initial retinal image into multiple image components, and the selective reconstitution of the components into the image that we perceive in our minds. Thus, the perceived image is a virtual construct created by the brain.
Needless to say, an ordinary person is entirely unaware that this processing is occurring, and that the perceived image is an artificial construct. The illusion evolved so perfectly that even an enlightened person may be “intellectually” aware but not really “emotionally” aware of what is happening.

Conclusions
The human brain is an object that came about as a result of biological evolution. This is just another way of saying that the human brain endows its host (the human body), and therefore itself, with a survival potential (in the context of the environment in which it evolved). To the extent that the human brain and the rest of the human body can be regarded as separate entities, they can be thought of as co-evolving and co-existing in a co-dependent symbiotic relationship.
Some aspects of survival entail the obvious, such as being able to reliably detect an approaching lion. So, the visual system needs to be more or less accurate with respect to external reality, at least in so far as it detects wavelengths of light within the visible spectrum.
On the other hand, there may be more abstract aspects of survival, such as motivation, which may very well be tied in to self-image and self-esteem. If the reality of our existence is, in fact, not very inspirational, then our survivability may very well depend upon our brains’ invention of more palatable explanatory stories or myths or memes into which we fit our experiences.
Personally, I view the human brain as exhibiting a mixture of two contrasting modes:
(1) a modest degree of functionality that just barely correlates with external reality
(2) a preponderance of self-serving invention
We are so impressed with our biological uniqueness that we are unable to attribute to the human brain the mediocre degree of accuracy with which
it functions. Instead, we grossly over-value the level of our brains’ functioning. This over-valuation is itself a product of the brain’s preponderance of self-serving invention – a dilemma of self-referentiality that may prove fatal in the long run. This is built into our brains via our genomes via biological evolution.
In the field of image processing, we know that the image is a distorted version of the true object [13]. We do not have a psychological problem admitting this. If we have a model of how the distortions occur, we can attempt to partially correct for them, and thus decrease the degree of distortion. We can also avoid over-interpreting the image, since we know that it is distorted.
Unfortunately, our unfounded over-reliance on the ideas emanating from the human brain stands in stark contrast to how engineers study image processing. We are so immersed in the invention that we do not suspect that it is anything other than reality. We have extraordinarily little inclination to develop an awareness of these inventions, and of freeing ourselves from the control that these inventions impose upon our lives. This is built into our brains by the encoding in our genomes, that, in turn, were “determined” (metaphorically speaking) by evolution. If we could more objectively evaluate the limitations of the human brain, then we could perhaps develop methods to partially compensate for them, and perhaps we could be more cautious in evaluating the (low) quality and (low) degree of validity of the ideas coming from our brains.
