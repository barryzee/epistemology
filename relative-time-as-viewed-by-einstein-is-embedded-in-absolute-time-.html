Motivation
In the previous two sections, we considered whether the past and the future are different or the same.
In the scenario where they are different, we explored whether the difference was physically manifested by a conformation change upon a material substrate S (yet to be discovered). Let us designate by R the rate at which the conformation designating “future” is changed into the conformation designating “past.”
In the scenario where they are the same, we explored whether the sameness was manifested by a “projector tape” that passed in front of a lens. In both cases, there is a process that is “time- dependent,” either a conformation change which has a finite rate R , or a movement of a “projector tape,” which again has a finite rate.

In terms of the metaphors for the passage of Einsteinian time t, dt/dT can be thought of as representing R or the rate of movement of projector film past a lens (Table 1). According to the Theory of Relativity, the rate of flow of Einsteinian time t depends on the circumstances. So, unlike Newtonian time T, Einsteinian time t does not flow at a constant rate. In the symbolism of calculus, dt/dT is not constant.
In the projector metaphor, we could imagine two projectors that run identical copies of the same film, but at speeds that differ from one another. The brain of the person viewing each projector might be calibrated so as to not notice that their projector is running at anything other than the “normal” speed, but an observer in T would be able to measure the difference in the speeds.
The passage of time as we experience it (known as subjective time) is not the same as the regular passage of time as measured by our clocks (objective time). The crucial difference is that subjective time can speed up and slow down, according to what we are experiencing. On a moment-by-moment scale, the rate at which time seems to pass is dictated by the rate of firing, or oscillation, of clusters of neurons. The faster they fire, the more events we register in any given second, giving us the impression that time lasts longer. Neuronal firing is controlled by neurotransmitters - excitatory ones speed it up, and inhibitory ones slow it down [3].
Remember that the events in the movies are already recorded on the films. There is simply the illusion that the part that has not yet been seen is in the future. In reality, it is already on the film, but we just haven’t seen it yet.

Evolutionary Consideration 1: Evolution Did not Give Us A Receptor to Sense T
Why can we not experience T? Well there are five generic reasons for a sense not to evolve:
• The technology required to implement the perception is not feasible within a biological system
• The chemical substances that are required to implement the perception are either non-existent, or are not available in sufficient amounts in the environment ( e.g., sea water) in which the ancestral organism evolved [4]
• The pathway for the sequence of random mutations that would be required is not feasible:
o The pathway goes through an intermediate state that is not survivable
o The required pathway is so improbable that it would take a lot longer than the current age of the Earth
• The sense would not confer a survival benefit to the organism
• The sense might reduce survivability by preventing us from creating the overlays and inventions that we need in order to survive

Evolutionary Consideration 2: Synchronization of the Rate of Brain Processing and the Rate of Flow of Einsteinian Time t
We know that R cannot be measured by Einsteinian time t, since R is the rate of flow of t, and you obviously cannot use t to measure itself. But R can be measured by Newtonian time T.
An identical argument applies to the flow of the hypothetical “projector time” (see Table 1).
We know that, in the case of real movie projectors, the engineering of the projector needs to meet certain requirements that are imposed by the rate at which the brain processes information, and the rate that is required for the brain to see the discrete frames as if they were a continuous motion. These were reviewed in the section “THE NATURE OF THE PRESENT.”
If the real projector were too slow, the brain would perceive an annoying delay and jerkiness. If the real projector were too fast, the brain would not be able to keep up, and so important information would constantly and continuously be lost. Fortunately, a real projector can meet these requirements.
On the other hand, in the case of the processes in Table 1, those rates had been pre-set by external reality, long before there were any life forms that had brains. So the brain must have evolved in response to the rates of the processes in Table 1. That is, evolution was able to synchronize the rate of brain functioning with R or the rate of passage of “projector time,” so as to an illusion of

The synchronization must involve at least three steps, namely the sensory input ( i.e. , the absorption of photons striking the retina, signal transduction via rhodopsin conformation changes, and the further brain processing including reconstruction of the pieces of the scene, interpretation of the reconstructed scene, and laying down of memory). This entire chain needs to happen at a rate that matches R or the rate of passage of “projector time.” If even one step in the chain is too slow, then our visual system would fall further and further behind, and we would being seeing things that had happened a long time ago rather than what is happening at the moment. Conversely, if the chain is too fast, then our visual experience would have gaps that would be very annoying and distracting.
Evolutionary Consideration 3:
Is the Perception of Time a Sense Without a Receptor?
Although psychologists believe that there is a neurological system governing the perception of time, it appears not to be associated with specific sensory pathways, but rather uses a highly distributed
system in the brain. Time perception therefore differs from our other
senses – sight, hearing, taste, smell, touch, even proprioception – since time cannot be directly perceived, and so must be “reconstructed” in some way by the brain [5].
Our sense of time seems to have originated as a product of human
evolution, and it is not a purely automatic or innate process, but a complex activity that we develop and actively learn as we grow. Humans are, as far as we know, the only animals to be consciously

aware of the passage of time and our own impermanence and mortality, and to have a consciousness of the past that is anything more than pure instinct and behavioural conditioning [5].
