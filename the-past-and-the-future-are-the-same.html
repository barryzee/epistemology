THE PAST AND THE FUTURE ARE THE SAME
No difference between past and future?
In the section “THE PAST AND THE FUTURE ARE DIFFERENT,” we considered a material substrate S (yet to be discovered) that could hold two distinct conformations of time. But the difficulty in finding S might indicate that, in external reality, past and future are not distinct states or conformations. Then we might explore whether it is the brain that “invented” the (possibly artificial) concepts of “past” and “future” (see for instance [1], [2]).
The speculation that there is no difference between past and future is not novel. All you need to do is google “is past the same as future in physics.” My primary interest in this question pertains to the focal point of this entire project, namely the biological evolution of the brain’s use of overlay and invention in a way that happened to result in survival.
We already know that some of the invented concepts, with which biological evolution endowed our brains, are very convincing. It is very hard for us to believe that certain things in actuality are not as they seems to us. Among the most difficult would be that, in external reality, there is no difference between past and future. This situation is in direct conflict with our own most intimate apparent experience, and is next to impossible to accept. It calls into question whether we do or not have free will.

An old-fashioned reel-to-reel movie tape as an apt analogy
Consider an old-fashioned reel-to-reel movie projector such as might have been commonly used in movie theaters. Table 2 shows the disposition of portions of the film as the movie is progressing.
Perhaps an old-fashioned reel-to-reel movie tape is an apt analogy to time. We are not moving through time, rather we are standing still. We are just waiting to see what happens to be in front of the projector lens from moment to moment. The future has already happened, but we must wait until the portion of the film is momentarily positioned in front of the lens of the projector in order to access it. An enterprising person might be able to peek ahead by pulling some of the tape off the reel.

When we see a movie for the first time, we have no idea what will happen later in the film. But it has already happened, it is recorded there in the film. It is not known to us (but please review items 3 and 4 in Table 1 of the section “THE PAST AND THE FUTURE ARE DIFFERENT”), but it is as un-mutable as the past. The difference is that we already know what happened in the past. We just need to wait for the future to appear in the lens.

The Order of Events
If time is not passing in physical reality, then there is no such thing as ordering events, no such thing as “event A caused event B.” Perhaps it is the brain has evolved so as to expect or insist on temporal cause and effect. The academic study of the theory of evolution certainly requires that. After all, a random mutation happens, and after that it is tested for survivability in the current environment. A mutation cannot be tested before it happens. Even without events actually happening, we can view them as being sequential frames in the projector film analogy. Just as a reminder, events in the future are already recorded on the film exactly as are events in the past.
But the frames are sequentially organized on the film, so this would in a sense comprise an ordering of before and after . Perhaps we could remove the idea of the events being ordered on the projector film, and the events could be stored randomly? In that case, what is it that gives them any order in our experience of them? Well, it could be the capacity of the brain to use its

experience to extrapolate and predict (see video [3] starting at 3:50) the next several tens of milliseconds (see video [3] starting at 6:15). If possibilities were stored as an associative array [4], the brain could use the keys of the array to retrieve an appropriate candidate “next moment.” That is, the apparent order is just our brain retrieving an event from a large array of possible events, such that the chosen event is consistent with our brain’s expectations of continuity and of “A causes B.”
A major problem with this scenario is that external reality is presumably the same for different people, so the retrieval of events from an associative array would need to function identically in each person. This is a little bit hard to swallow, so I would tentatively suggest that the events on the projector film are in fact arranged in temporal order.
