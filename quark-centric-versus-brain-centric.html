QUARK-CENTRIC VERSUS BRAIN-CENTRIC
I am of such an age that when I took science in school, we learned about electrons and protons, but not about quarks. No matter what substance an object is made of, if you dig deep enough, its protons and neutrons are made of quarks held together by gluons (more or less). This is the mystery substance. It is no coincidence that Superman was called Quark Kent.
This manuscript was written from a human brain-centric point of view. That is, the frame of reference was a hypothetical human brain that functions in a rational manner. That is a natural point of view for a human to take.
However, that point of view may be narrow-minded and inherently biased. Perhaps it would be less myopic to take a different point of view, say the point of view of a quark. The human brain might come with lot of baggage, but a quark is presumably among the most basic of objects, as free as possible of baggage. The human brain is a very complex construct, and the way it functions might very well reflect a bias associated with its structure and/or its evolutionary history. That is not likely to be an issue with a quark.
For instance, my own brain might feel comfortable with a conclusion reached using accurate logic. But what if (the system of) logic itself is flawed? There might just happen to be something about the human brain that makes logic appear attractive, even though logic is (for the sake of argument) basically flawed.
There are two properties of the human brain that are worth mentioning:
(1) Human brains are more or less closely related clones of one another. For instance, if you were to perform CAT scans or PET scans or dissections (with the exception of instances of diseased brains) all brains look more or less the same, except for fine details. The locations of neurotransmitters and neuroreceptors are likewise pretty much the same. The same is true of abstract functioning – vast
numbers of brains have similar preferences for types of beliefs, or types of music. The differences can be characterized by (2) just below.
(2) Any given property (either tangible or abstract) of the human brain (for example, physical size, intellect, etc. ) can be represented mathematically/statistically as a distribution, across all brains, very often by a ‘bell- curve,’ also known as a ‘normal distribution.’ This distribution is characterized by a middle section that contains the bulk of items that are pretty much like the expected value, and tails that contain ‘outliers.’
Logic or reason might be thought of as a property of human brains, in the sense of (1) and (2) above. Of course, some human brains are more logical than others, overall likely following a normal distribution if we could test and quantify some seven billion brains (or perhaps randomly sample a representative subset). Most of this manuscript concerned itself with differentiating the rational versus the non-rational.
But it is just possible that a preference for the rational, albeit widespread among our normal distribution, might be a cloned feature that happens to be incorrect, or perhaps makes no sense at all in the deepest analysis. After all, the human brain reflects a complex structuring of countless quarks that achieved its configuration by the sketchy nonlinear happenstance process of biological evolution. Would anyone in their ‘right mind’ really count on this hit and miss process to generate a human brain that was good at anything other than its own immediate survivability?
Perhaps it makes more sense (that is, it is more fundamental) to look at things from the point of view of quarks than from the point of view of the human brain.
An immediate impediment is that, to the human brain, quarks are pretty boring. Human brains can do a lot of things, like compose music or write books. Quarks cannot do these things.

If a brain is ultimately made of quarks (and gluons), can a brain be more than the sum of its parts?
Well, a novel is made up of a lot of letters. Is a novel more than the sum of its letters? The novel can tell a story that has ‘redeeming social value,’ but letters cannot. However, upon closer analysis, the novel is written by a human brain so as to encode something that is interpretable to another human brain. So, the real content of a novel is just a reflection of human value judgements etc., which may not be as informative as we might wish. Perhaps there is just as much ( i.e., as little) information in a pile of random letters as in a novel that was designed to guide our emotions or thoughts in accord with someone’s agenda (not meant as a pejorative, just that the writer had some goal in mind, albeit benevolent)?
Likewise, a brain is not a random pile of quarks. It is a very special pile of quarks. The quarks in a brain got there as the end result of biological evolution. This assemblage met the criterion of survivability under the prevailing conditions. If the Earth had a different mass, then its gravitational attraction would be different. And so would the oxygen within Earth’s atmosphere, the amount of which is determined by the Earth’s gravitational attraction. Then there are two alternative outcomes for all aspects of the human body, including the brain:
(1) The materials in the Earth’s crust that were needed for constructing the human body might not be adequate for composing a human body that can function under the altered gravitational attraction and the altered oxygen level.
(2) The biochemistry of the human body (particularly energy metabolism) might be way better or way worse than it is now, impinging on the structure and functioning of the brain.
Ultimately, the brain is the end result of biological evolution, whose ‘goal’ is not perfection but survivability. For example, our inability to see wavelengths of light outside of what we term ‘the visible’ is not perfection, as we are failing to see all of what is there; but this limitation leads to enhanced survivability.
Likewise, aspects of brain functioning that lead to e.g. , racial prejudice, could be deemed as falling short of perfection; but the underlying processes of categorization and generalization (when working properly) are essential for survivability.
In stark contrast, quarks are considered to be the most basic elements of matter, perhaps indivisible. A quark does not have ‘parts’ that evolve in a certain way; a quark just is what it is. There is no sense in which a quark could be said to have evolved. For a quark, there is no tradeoff between perfection and survivability. The downside is that there is no meaningful (to our way of thinking) interaction between a quark and the external world, that can only happen for a structure that has multiple parts.
Let us consider certain aspects of an important human emotion, namely love. The perception of love exists within the brain, but it does not exist in and of itself in objective external reality. This essential human emotion is mediated by certain hormones that are released by the vanishingly tiny paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus in conjunction with the neurotransmitter dopamine.
As tiny as this nucleus is, it is vastly larger than a quark. There are many ‘moving parts’ to the PVN, all of which are essential to producing its hormones at the right times and in the right quantities. Variations in these processes would not lead to the expected or desired outcome.
Our experience of life would be very threadbare if there were no sense of love or human connections. Yet, this is produced by our subjective response to the hormones and neurotransmitters. For a quark, there is no such thing; a quark does not have the level of organization that is needed. A quark has no expectations (and therefore no disappointments or exaltations), a quark cannot suffer from boredom or from loneliness, or from lack of connection to other quarks.
Perhaps we have a choice of frame of reference that we live by. Using an intact, healthy, normal human (brain) as the frame of reference, we elevate love to the highest levels. A parent would not hesitate to sacrifice him/her self to save the life of their own infant. People who live without love or human connection are very lonely indeed.
It would take a very exceptional individual to prefer the quark as his or her frame of reference. The quark is way more fundamental (and honest) than the human brain as a frame of reference. This person would reject love as being a rather arbitrary or perhaps whimsical overlay on top of objective external reality.
In physics, according to the Special Theory of Relativity, there is no preferred frame of reference (‘everything is relative’). It is tempting to apply the same analogy to the dilemma of brain versus quark. Is one of them the preferred frame of reference (or perhaps something else is the ‘correct’ frame of reference, or perhaps there is no preferred frame of reference?).
As an exercise for the student, you can write a similar narrative regarding the fact that murder is considered by humans to be a sin (even elevated to be one of the ten commandments!).
